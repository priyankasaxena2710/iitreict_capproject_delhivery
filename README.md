# Delhivery Last-Mile Delay Intelligence Pipeline

Capstone project, AI & ML Certification Program, E&ICT Academy IIT Roorkee.
All data is synthetic (produced by `generate_data.py`); Delhivery is only the setting for the decisions.
One connected pipeline answers three questions: how long will a parcel take (Part B), which parcels will
miss their promise (Part C), and which hubs need which playbook (Part D).

## How to run

From a fresh clone, on the `main` branch, in the repository root:

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python generate_data.py          # prints: 24 1224 48
python part_a_clean_eda.py       # data/modelling_table.csv + Part A outputs
python part_b_regression.py      # Part B outputs
python part_c_classification.py  # Part C outputs
python part_d_segments.py        # hub profile, segments, separation, tier check
python package_model.py          # models/delay_model.pkl + threshold, feature order, impute values
python predict_delay.py          # outputs/new_shipment_actions.csv
```

`models/delay_model.pkl` is regenerated by running `python package_model.py`. A pickle can only be loaded
reliably by the scikit-learn version that wrote it, which is the version pinned in `requirements.txt`; if your
installed versions differ, re-run `package_model.py` instead of loading the committed pickle.
Run every script from the repository root (the scripts import `src/shipment_prep.py`).

## Data findings

Source: `outputs/part_a_delay_by_group.csv`, `outputs/part_a_correlations.csv`, `outputs/part_a_checks.csv`.
After removing 24 duplicates there are 1,200 parcels, of which 278 were delayed (overall delay rate 0.2317).

1. **Express is delayed most often.** Express parcels miss their promise at a rate of 0.4897 (119 of 243),
   against 0.2056 for Standard (132 of 642) and 0.0857 for Economy (27 of 315). Express has the tightest
   promise (36 hours), so it has the least slack.
2. **Delay rate climbs steeply with hub tier.** It rises from 0.0400 at Tier 1 to 0.2150 at Tier 2 and
   0.4400 at Tier 3, so Tier 3 is eleven times as likely to be late as Tier 1.
3. **Delay rate explodes with distance.** It goes from 0.0219 in the 0-200 km band to 0.1368 (201-400),
   0.4364 (401-600) and 0.8523 for 601+ km, so most parcels on the longest lanes are late.
4. **Strongest correlations with `delivery_hours`** (ranked by absolute value, excluding itself):
   `promised_hours` (0.8372), `distance_km` (0.4013) and `n_stops` (0.3247).
5. **Why `declared_value_inr` was log-transformed.** The raw filled column is strongly right-skewed (mean
   INR 1,533.05 versus median INR 1,121.50), whereas after the log the mean (7.0277) and median (7.0224)
   almost coincide, giving a near-symmetric feature.

## Model results (regression)

Source: `outputs/part_b_*.csv`. Split: 840 train / 180 validation / 180 test. The scaler is fitted on training
rows only: the scaled training columns have a largest absolute mean of 0.0, while the scaled test rows show
0.2187, proving the test rows did not influence the scaler.

* **Baseline.** Predicting the training mean gives validation R² of -0.0212 (RMSE 18.8552 hours). An R² of about
  zero means it explains none of the variation; any useful model must beat it.
* **Winner on validation.** Linear regression, with validation RMSE 4.8912 hours (R² 0.9313). Gradient boosting
  is almost identical (RMSE 4.9165, R² 0.9306), then random forest (5.5095) and the decision tree (6.2796).
* **Bias and variance (tree depth).** Depths 1, 2 and 3 are high-bias: train and validation errors are both high
  (depth 1: train 11.6334, validation 12.1324, a gap of only 0.50). The best depth is 6 (validation RMSE
  6.2796). Deep trees are high-variance: depth 12 has train 1.3613 versus validation 7.3540 (gap 5.99), and with
  no limit train RMSE is 0.0 while validation is 7.8368 (gap 7.84), so they memorise noise.
* **Learning rate.** 0.1 was best (validation R² 0.9306). At 0.01 (R² 0.7127) 100 trees take steps that are too
  small and the model is still under-fitted. At 1.0 (R² 0.8440) every step overshoots, so the ensemble bounces
  around the optimum instead of settling.
* **Cross-validation.** On the 1,020 training-plus-validation rows (one shared `KFold`), linear regression has
  mean R² 0.9261 (std 0.0048) and gradient boosting has 0.9353 (std 0.0045). The ranking disagrees with the
  validation ranking. I would ship **gradient boosting**: 5-fold CV uses five times more evaluation data than a
  single 180-row validation set, its advantage there (+0.0092 R²) is larger than its validation deficit
  (0.0253 hours of RMSE, negligible), and the data contain interactions (sale x Express, distance x rain) that a
  linear model can only approximate. The pre-declared rule in the brief scores the validation winner, linear
  regression, once on the test rows: test RMSE 4.5235 hours, R² 0.9493.

## Model results (classification)

Source: `outputs/part_c_*.csv`. Stratified split: 840 / 180 / 180 rows with 195 / 42 / 41 delayed parcels.

* **Blind baseline.** Predicting "not delayed" for everything has validation accuracy 0.7667 (138 TN, 42 FN),
  but recall 0.0: it never catches a delayed parcel, which is the entire purpose of the model.
* **Models at the 0.5 rule.** Logistic regression F1 0.8046, decision tree (depth 6) F1 0.7470, default random
  forest F1 0.7848; all have accuracy of at least 0.8833, above the blind baseline.
* **Noise columns.** The five bookkeeping columns hold 0.2245 of the forest's importance in total, even though
  they carry no signal (a forest splits on noise when it can). By F-score all five fall outside the top 7: their
  best rank is 8 and their largest F-score is 4.5847, whereas the weakest of the top seven is 36.7281.
* **Choosing k.** Validation F1 is 0.6988 (k=5), 0.8333 (k=7), 0.7857 (k=10), 0.8049 (k=12) and 0.7848 (k=17).
  k=7 is best; it keeps exactly the seven real signal columns, and k=5 drops two of them and loses accuracy.
* **Tuning.** Grid search (best CV accuracy 0.8952, validation F1 0.7750) and randomized search (0.8905, 0.7848)
  did **not** beat the untuned 7-feature forest (validation F1 0.8333). With only 42 delayed validation parcels,
  one parcel changes F1 by several points, so differences between configurations are mostly noise; tuning on a
  180-row validation set risks over-fitting the validation set. The final model is therefore the default
  forest on the 7 selected features (`chosen_from = default_forest_same_7_features`).
* **Alert threshold.** The rule picks the highest threshold with validation recall of at least 0.80. At 0.5 the
  validation recall is 0.8333 and precision 0.7955; at 0.6 precision rises to 0.8889 but recall falls to 0.7619,
  so 0.5 is chosen. Going lower (0.3) would catch 0.9286 but precision drops to 0.6964.
* **Final test (scored once).** At threshold 0.5: precision 0.6889, recall 0.7561, F1 0.7209 (TP 31, FN 10,
  FP 14). Test recall is lower than the validation recall of 0.8333 and misses the 0.80 aim. With only 41
  delayed test parcels this gap is about three parcels, so it is within sampling noise, but it warns that
  the 0.80 recall promise is not guaranteed in production.

## Operations recommendation

* **Escalate segment (9 hubs, delay rate 0.4600 on average):** Nagpur (HUB13, 0.46), Kochi (HUB15, 0.36),
  Patna (HUB17, 0.46), Ranchi (HUB18, 0.38), Guwahati (HUB19, 0.62), Raipur (HUB20, 0.48), Bhubaneswar
  (HUB21, 0.40), Siliguri (HUB23, 0.44) and Agartala (HUB24, 0.54). Segment means: Standard has 7 hubs at 0.0257,
  Watch has 8 hubs at 0.1550.
* **Tier labels that mislead** (`tier_vs_segment_mismatch.csv`): Kolkata HUB07 is Tier 1 but behaves like a Watch
  hub; Nagpur HUB13 and Kochi HUB15 are Tier 2 but behave like Escalate hubs; Dehradun HUB22 is Tier 3 but behaves
  like a Watch hub. Use the behaviour-based segment, not the tier label, to decide which playbook a hub gets;
  the tier label is a reasonable first guess but is wrong for 4 of 24 hubs.
* **Alert threshold 0.5.** From `part_c_final_test.csv`: of every 10 flagged parcels about 7 are really delayed
  (precision 0.6889), and of every 10 delayed parcels about 8 are caught (recall 0.7561, i.e. 7.6).
* **New parcels.** Of the 48 new parcels, 8 were flagged; 6 of them sit in Escalate hubs and need a
  hub-manager escalation, and 2 get a proactive customer call.
* **Why K = 3.** `part_d_separation.csv` shows the across-over-within ratio rising from 2.8940 (K=2) to 5.3928
  (K=3), 6.3047 (K=4) and 6.8866 (K=5). Operations runs exactly three playbooks, and the K=4 and K=5 solutions
  contain clusters of only 4 hubs (sizes 8-7-5-4) and 3 and 2 hubs (8-7-4-3-2), too small to carry their own
  playbook, while K=3 gives sizes 9-8-7.

## Production plan

**(a) Production-readiness characteristics mapped to the repository**

| Characteristic | Item in this repository |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Held-out test evaluation in `part_c_final_test.csv` (F1 0.7209) and `part_b_metrics.csv` |
| Reliability | `clean_shipments` handles duplicates, sentinels, missing values and casing; `reload_check` proves the saved model works |
| Reproducibility | Pinned `requirements.txt`, fixed `random_state` values, committed generator, scripts run in the README order |
| Performance (speed) | A 100-tree forest on 7 features scores the 48 new parcels in well under a second, and scaling is linear in rows |
| Maintainability | Shared `src/shipment_prep.py`, one script per Part, config-driven `package_model.py` |
| Consistency | `predict_delay.py` imports the same `clean_shipments`/`build_features` used in training, with stored medians and `feature_order.csv` |
| Monitorability | `new_shipment_actions.csv` stores probability, flag and action per parcel for later comparison with outcomes |

**(b) Quantities to monitor, with numeric triggers**

* Weekly delayed share of delivered parcels: the historical rate is 0.2317; investigate if it moves outside
  0.15 to 0.30 for two consecutive weeks.
* Weekly share of parcels flagged: investigate if it moves more than 5 percentage points away from the
  rate seen on the validation set (0.2444 at threshold 0.5: 44 of 180 parcels) for two weeks.
* Recall on parcels whose outcome is now known (scored weekly): retrain if it falls below 0.65 over a
  rolling window of at least 40 delayed parcels (test recall was 0.7561).
* Input drift: mean distance_km or n_stops shifting by more than 10% from the training means.

**(c) Retraining and the sale window.** Retrain monthly on all delivered parcels with known outcomes, and
immediately if any trigger above fires. The 15-28 September sale window (`sale_event_flag`) must be compared
with the previous sale window (or the same kind of event), not against ordinary weeks, because sale-time delay
rates are structurally higher (Express parcels get an extra 12 hours in the simulation) and would trigger false alarms.
Data drift here would look like a change in lane distances or stops per route, new hubs or service types,
a shift in the share of COD or Express parcels, or the delay rate at a given distance changing.

## Limitations

1. **Synthetic data.** The relationships were created by a generator, so real performance will differ and the
   clean signal here (R² above 0.93) is unrealistically high.
2. **Few delayed parcels in test.** The test set has only 41 delayed parcels, so precision, recall and F1 have wide
   uncertainty; one parcel moves recall by about 2.4 percentage points.
3. **Threshold tuned on a single validation split.** The 0.5 threshold and the choice of the untuned forest were
   made on 42 delayed validation parcels and may not generalise.
4. **Hub segments rest on 50 parcels per hub and only 24 hubs**, and K-Means results for K=4 and K=5 depend on seed and order.
5. **One time window.** Only 90 days of data (July to September 2026) were used, so seasonality outside this window is unseen.
